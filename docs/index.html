Abstract

Amplification, not substitution — and a test that lied about its own confidence.

This project tests whether the stability–instability paradox — developed for nuclear deterrence — generalises to conventional precision-strike capability: guided munitions, combat drones, and networked targeting. A 192-country panel triangulates SIPRI, UCDP, V-Dem, World Bank, and Correlates of War data to ask three linked questions about composition, mechanism, and structure.

RQ1 · Composition

Capability predicts more of everything — especially lethality

Two-way fixed-effects panel regression finds no substitution: higher capability is tied to significantly more war and minor-conflict participation, and most strongly to deadlier conflict once it occurs (β=2.03, p<.001).

RQ2 · Lead-Lag

A textbook test was miscalibrated — and its "signal" evaporates

The standard Dumitrescu–Hurlin test nominally flags 8 of 12 weapon→conflict cells. Under a corrected circular-shift null with FDR correction, zero survive — confirmed independently by an out-of-sample forecasting benchmark.

RQ3 · Archetypes

Four conflict-capability archetypes, half provably stable

K-Means clustering (n=610 country-epochs) recovers four archetypes that predict future conflict out-of-sample (F=33.6, p<.001) — but only 2 of 4 clear a correctly calibrated bootstrap-stability threshold.

Synthesis

Every strong first-pass claim needed qualification — and got sharper for it.

Read across all three research questions, a single thread connects results that on the surface look unrelated: nearly every claim this project could have made on a first pass turned out to need correction once tested properly — and in every case the correction made the finding more precise, not weaker.
